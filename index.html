Through the Lens: Tokyo Sightseeing & Digital Footprints

An interactive data story exploring tourist mobility across Tokyo and the photography equipment capturing digital memories.

Author: Rachel Chiew Dhian Lin
Unit: FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2
Date: October 2026
Data Sources: tokyo_clean.csv (Flickr Metadata) & camera_dataset.csv

Project Overview & Context

Who: Designed for an audience interested in urban tourism analytics, travel mobility, and photography technology.
What: A single-page data visualization dashboard combining spatial geotagged photo footprints across Tokyo with technical camera specs.
Why: To uncover where visitors congregate in Tokyo, how individuals move between landmarks, and how camera hardware (ISO, weight, price, resolution) supports urban photography.
How: Built using Vega-Lite with 12 non-repeating advanced visualization idioms layered over custom geographic TopoJSON land boundaries.

Act I: Spatial & Geographic Dynamics

Every day, thousands of visitors explore Tokyo, leaving behind digital footprints via geotagged photographs. Act I maps these footprints across four distinct spatial perspectives.

Map 1: Sightseeing Distribution by Region

Tokyo operates as a distinct collection of wards. By classifying photo longitudes into municipal zones, over 65% of all geotagged photos concentrate within the Central Core Wards (Shinjuku, Shibuya, Minato), while Waterfront and Western zones serve secondary excursion routes.

Key Insight: The Central Core Wards host peak photo density, driven by iconic landmarks like Shibuya Crossing, Tokyo Tower, and Shinjuku.

Map 12: Individual Tourist Trajectory & Destination

Filtering by tourist ID reveals individual daily routes. Some visitors stay within tight localized hubs, while others execute cross-city journeys. The red diamond highlights each tourist's final recorded photo destination.

Key Insight: Use the interactive dropdown menu to trace individual travel routes and locate their final destination diamond.

Map 2: Proportional Spatial Symbol Map

Scaling photo markers by spatial coordinate magnitude illustrates structural spatial weight. Rather than spreading evenly, tourist activities cling tightly to major transport arteries framing central Tokyo.

Key Insight: Bubble sizes visually emphasize how tourist activity concentrates along the circular Yamanote train line.

Map 3: Spatial Density Grid Map

Aggregating photos into 2D geographic grid cells eliminates point overlap. Continuous Viridis coloring reveals two primary super-clusters: Shinjuku-Shibuya in the west and Ginza-Asakusa in the east.

Key Insight: Shinjuku and Shibuya grid cells record peak densities exceeding 400 photos per cell.

Act II: Equipment Performance & Technology

Behind every photograph captured on Tokyo's streets is an optical instrument. Act II evaluates how price, weight, sensor resolution, and exposure specs shape consumer gear choices.

Chart 4: Price vs ISO Sensitivity (Jitter Plot)

Dispersing raw camera bodies across ISO sensitivity bins reveals that entry-level gear ($300–$700) tops out at ISO 6,400, whereas professional bodies ($2,000+) push beyond ISO 25,600 for nighttime street shooting.

Key Insight: Cameras with ISO limits above 25,600 command a price premium, averaging over $2,200 per body.

Chart 5: Price Distribution Across Brands (Scatter Plot)

Plots individual camera body prices across major manufacturers on a logarithmic scale, highlighting the spread from budget point-and-shoot models to high-end flagship systems.

Key Insight: Premium brands maintain tight pricing clusters in the $1,500–$3,500 bracket, while mainstream brands cover the full $100–$2,000 range.

Chart 6: Median Price Evolution (Slope Graph)

Connects historical median camera price points across consecutive release years, illustrating market valuation shifts and technology adoption phases.

Key Insight: Average camera prices spiked alongside digital sensor adoption before stabilizing as manufacturing matured.

Chart 7: Lens Zoom vs Price Density (Heatmap)

Aggregates camera models into 2D binned cells comparing wide focal lengths against price brackets to expose structural manufacturing concentrations.

Key Insight: The vast majority of consumer camera models concentrate in the standard 35–38mm wide-angle range under $1,000.

Chart 8: Model Releases per Year (Lollipop Chart)

Ranks annual camera model release totals using high-contrast stem-and-dot lines to depict historical product launch momentum across the digital camera boom.

Key Insight: Annual product releases grew steeply between 1998 and 2006, peaking as digital cameras replaced film.

Chart 9: Top Storage Formats (Bar Chart)

Compares memory card format support counts across camera models, illustrating the industry-wide transition toward standardized SD and CompactFlash media.

Key Insight: Standard SD and CompactFlash formats account for over 75% of total card slot adoption across cataloged models.

Chart 10: Average Price by Brand (Dot Plot)

Ranks camera manufacturers along a clean horizontal axis based on mean body retail price to highlight market positioning strategies.

Key Insight: Specialized manufacturers command average price points above $800, whereas mass-market brands average $150–$400.

Chart 11: Resolution Evolution Trend (Connected Dot Plot)

Connects historical maximum resolution milestones chronologically, demonstrating steady optical sensor advancements across consecutive release years.

Key Insight: Maximum effective resolution grew exponentially from under 1,000 pixels in 1994 to over 3,000 pixels by 2006.
