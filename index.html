Through the Lens: Tokyo

Where do visitors point their cameras in Tokyo, and what kind of camera is in their hands? Follow the photos first, then the gear behind them.

Planning a trip to Tokyo? Millions of visitors share geotagged photos online, and each photo quietly records where its owner stood. Put together, those photos show which parts of the city draw the crowds and how people move between them. This page then turns to the cameras themselves: what they cost, how they have changed, and which brands sit where on the market. It is written for curious travellers and photography fans, so no statistics background is needed.

Quick terms

Geotag
A photo's saved location, so it can be placed on a map.
Megapixels
How many million dots make up a photo. More megapixels means finer detail.
Zoom (focal length)
How far a lens can bring a faraway scene closer.
Log scale
An axis where each step multiplies the value, so cheap and expensive items fit on one chart.

Act I · The city

Where Tokyo Gets Photographed

Start with the big picture. The left map places one dot for every photo, so you can see exactly where people stood. Because central Tokyo is so busy that dots start to overlap, the right map counts the photos in each 1 km square and shades them, which makes the busiest streets easy to spot.

Every photo as a dot

Each dot is one geotagged photo (10,000 in total). Darker patches are where many photos overlap. Hover over a dot to see its position.

Photo density in 1 km squares

Darker squares hold more photos. Squares with fewer than 3 photos are hidden to keep the map clean.

The Ten Busiest Areas and the Moves Between Them

Zoom in on the centre. The left map sizes a circle by how many photos were taken in each of the ten busiest areas, which together hold about 46% of all photos. The right map then follows visitors: an arrow joins two areas when a visitor photographed one and then the other, and thicker arrows mean more visitors made that move.

Ten busiest photo areas

Bigger circles mean more photos. Each area is a patch of about 2 km across; names are approximate. Hover over a circle for its name and share of all photos.

Popular moves between hotspots

Numbers match the circles on the left: 1 Ginza–Tsukiji, 2 Ueno, 3 Tokyo Station–Akihabara, 4 Harajuku, 5 Imperial Palace, 6 Asakusa, 7 Shinjuku, 8 Roppongi, 9 Shibuya, 10 Shinagawa. Arrows point from the first area photographed to the next. Only moves between these ten areas are counted (866 in total); use the slider to show only the busiest moves.

Photos pile up in a few central hotspots, and the busiest moves link close neighbours such as Ginza, Tokyo Station and Ueno, or Harajuku and Shibuya.

Act II · The camera

What Does a Camera Cost?

Behind every photo is a camera, and cameras vary enormously. Some are simple, budget-friendly models. Others are professional bodies built for demanding conditions. The first chart plots every camera by release year and price, with bigger circles for more megapixels. The second compares how prices are spread within each brand. Both use a log scale, so one step up means many times the price.

Camera price by release year

Each circle is one camera, and bigger circles have more megapixels. Circles are spread slightly sideways within their year so that cameras released in the same year don’t hide each other.

Price range within each brand

Each box covers the middle half of a brand's prices, and the dark line is its typical (median) price. Dots are unusually high or low prices. Only brands with at least 15 models and five or more different prices are shown, because the others have too little price variety to compare.

How Cameras Changed Over Time

Cameras have not stood still. Across the years covered by the dataset, manufacturers released new models, sharper sensors arrived, and prices moved. These three charts follow that story: what the typical camera cost each year, how many new models appeared, and how image resolution grew.

Median price evolution

The median is the middle price, so a few extreme models do not distort it.

Model releases per year

Each stem is one year. New releases climbed from a handful in the mid-1990s to 163 models in 2007.

Resolution evolution trend

Resolution is the number of pixels in a photo. Following the maximum resolution year by year shows how quickly sensors improved. A sharper sensor lets a traveller crop into a skyline shot or print a large photo of Shibuya at night without it turning blurry.

What Is in the Camera Bag?

Finally, the details that shape how a camera feels to use: how far the lens zooms, which memory cards it takes, and how each brand positions itself on price. For a traveller, zoom decides whether you can capture Tokyo Tower from across the park, and storage decides which cards you need to pack.

Lens zoom vs price density

Darker areas show where many cameras share a similar zoom and price.

Memory included with the camera

Each square is 1% of the camera models that come with a memory card. Almost a third include a 16 MB card, and the five most common sizes cover 71%.

Cheapest to priciest model by brand

Each line runs from a brand’s cheapest model (grey dot) to its most expensive (black dot), and the orange diamond marks its average price. Brands are ranked by average price, so those at the top lean towards premium equipment while those at the bottom target everyday, budget-minded buyers. Only brands with at least 15 models and more than one price are shown.

Putting It Together

The photos show that Tokyo is not photographed evenly: visitors gather in a few hotspots and spread out along routes between them. The camera data shows that the tools used to capture those moments range from simple to professional, and have improved in resolution and variety over time.

Whether you are choosing where to point your lens or which lens to buy, these charts give you a starting point for planning your own trip through Tokyo.
